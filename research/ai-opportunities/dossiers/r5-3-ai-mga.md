# R5-3: Hard-Class Product Liability MGA (with trade credit #26 as a later module)

**One-liner:** An AI-native MGA that scores and monitors every SKU of a consumer-goods brand (listing, injury words in reviews, CPSC recalls, certifications, where cells and ingredients come from) so it can pick the good risks in hard-to-place product-liability classes, quote in minutes, and turn renewal into loss prevention.

**Managing-partner verdict:** A real, venture-scale template, but it is not 70+ for anyone yet. The steelman is right that SKU-level risk is visible and unpriced. The skeptics are right on two points: hard classes already have specialist program administrators, and fronting capacity is tightening in 2026. Best routes are **Profile A (59)**, a funded team built around a credentialed underwriter and a capacity partner, and, just behind, **Profile C (58)**: buy an existing specialty products program and AI-enable it. Lifting A past 65 requires a signed fronting or quota-share term sheet before the seed closes.

## Thesis

Small consumer brands buy product liability because something forces them to. Amazon requires it once a seller passes $10K in gross sales in a month (verified), and retail vendor agreements require it too (not verified). Underwriters price by class code times revenue, check the product list roughly once, and watch nothing during the term. Yet each SKU leaves public, machine-readable risk signals. An MGA that scores and monitors those signals can choose risks better in classes incumbents avoid (lithium and e-mobility, supplements, cosmetics, juvenile products), quote faster than brokers, and lower losses during the term. The cyber parallel is Coalition: outside-in scanning plus monitoring. Coalition reports, as a vendor claim, that monitored clients file 73% fewer claims.

## Workflow today

- **Micro sellers:** Amazon notifies a seller at $10K/month and gives 30 days to show coverage. Most buy through the Amazon Insurance Accelerator (Marsh panel: Chubb, Hiscox, Liberty, Markel, Travelers, Harborway/Spinnaker) or digital agencies (Insurance Canopy, Thimble, Assureful). Rates are close to flat by category, and Amazon pays valid claims under $1,000.
- **Growing brands ($1-50M revenue):** a retail broker collects an ACORD application, product list, revenue by class, loss runs and retailer contract terms. Standard classes go to admitted carriers. Hard classes go through wholesalers (Amwins, RT Specialty, CRC) to E&S markets or specialist programs. Placement takes weeks, renewal is annual with a sales audit, and nobody monitors SKUs during the term.
- **Claims:** rare but severe (burns, lithium fires, choking), long-tail, mostly occurrence form, with years before losses are known.

## Wedge, then path to scale

1. **Months 0-12, agency:** a free SKU risk report (paste a storefront link, get per-SKU flags and a fix list). Place 2-3 hard classes through wholesalers into existing E&S markets at 10-15% commission. Target $5-15M placed premium and a large submission dataset. Contribution is negative in this phase.
2. **Years 1-3, delegated authority:** fronting paper plus a quota-share reinsurer, with the reinsurer ideally an equity investor (SiriusPoint played this role for Honeycomb). Target $30-100M GWP across 3-5 classes and $8-25M revenue.
3. **Years 3-6:** standard-class brands plus recall, cargo, umbrella and cyber. Embed in 3PLs, TikTok Shop, Walmart Marketplace and retailer compliance portals. Target $150-350M GWP.
4. **Years 6-9:** $400-500M+ GWP, about $100M+ revenue, and an option to take risk or become a carrier (Corgi's path).

**Sequencing:** start with supplements, cosmetics and juvenile products. Add lithium only after capacity is secured, because one fire cluster can cost the MGA its only capacity partner.

## Steelman summary (bull, about 68 for A)

SKU risk is visible but nobody underwrites it, much like cyber around 2017. Regulation is creating clean signals: NYC's UL mandate coincided with lithium-fire deaths falling from 18 to 6 to 1, California made UL 2849 mandatory in 2026, and CPSC has proposed a federal standard. Capital is ready (95.2% of Q1 2026 insurtech funding went to AI companies; Accelerant's IPO shows MGA paper exists at scale). Selection metrics and in-term actions can win quota shares before losses develop. The bull concedes the capacity gap is smaller than claimed. The pitch is better selection and monitoring, not "the only market."

## Skeptic summary (bear, about 56 for A)

- **Competition and go-to-market:** the hard classes already have program owners with loss history. Wholesalers decide placement, so a free report reaches the brand but not the bind. In E&S, the bottleneck is finding capacity, not speed. A CPSC standard turns certification into table stakes.
- **Feasibility:** AM Best cut its delegated-authority outlook to Stable and notes five fronting carriers have left or scaled back. Hybrid fronting demands risk retention or collateral, so "capital-light" does not hold. Fake UL certificates are common, so certification credits invite fraud. Scraped signals capture frequency, not the tail. In-term monitoring may create a loss-control duty and E&O exposure.

## What's good

- Real information asymmetry: SKU-level signals are public, structured and unused in pricing.
- Proven template and investor appetite (Honeycomb $275M GWP, Nirvana $1.5B valuation, Corgi $630M).
- Certification-linked credits give the brand an action that lowers premium, which makes insurance a compliance relationship and reduces churn.
- A retailer win (Walmart, Target, Home Depot) creates an urgent, higher-premium buying moment.
- Monitoring data is a moat that compounds, and it opens SaaS and adjacent-line revenue.
- Profile C has a credible buy-and-build path: a fragmented set of specialty programs.

## What's bad

- Capacity is the scarce input, and the 2026 fronting market is tightening, with demands for collateral and risk-sharing.
- Wedge classes are contested by programs that already hold carrier ties and loss history.
- Wholesalers gatekeep. Going direct conflicts with them, and going through them weakens the speed edge.
- Long tail: no loss-ratio proof for 3-4 years, and one catastrophic cluster can end the capacity relationship.
- The model is easy to copy (Harborway, Next, Canopy). Certification signals can be faked, and a federal standard would commoditize them.
- Getting to $100M takes 7-10 years and depends on adjacent lines.

## Best founder profile and why

**Profile A, built specifically around capacity:** a technical CEO, a products or casualty underwriter cofounder with a book and relationships, and a reinsurer or fronting carrier on the cap table from day one. Only this setup combines underwriting credibility, money for the scoring engine, and runway through the agency phase. **Profile C** is a close second and carries less risk: buy a supplement or cosmetics program, plug in the SKU engine to improve selection and speed, then add more programs. Its ceiling is lower and its execution risk is concentrated in change-of-control consent. Profile 0 should not attempt this.

## First 30 days

1. Interview 10 wholesale brokers (Amwins, RT, CRC product desks) and 5 hard-class program administrators about decline reasons, time-to-bind, and whether a SKU risk report would change placement.
2. Hold capacity conversations with 3-5 fronting carriers or reinsurers (Accelerant, SiriusPoint, MS Transverse, Builders). Learn collateral and retention terms and whether a cofounder's book clears their bar.
3. Recruit or lock in the products-underwriter cofounder.
4. Build a SKU-scoring prototype on 500 supplement, cosmetics and juvenile listings, backtested against CPSC recalls and SaferProducts reports to see whether the score flags the SKUs that were later recalled.
5. For Profile C, screen 10-20 specialty products programs and agencies (NutraInsure-type) for size, carrier contracts and consent clauses.
6. Go/no-go: at least one capacity partner sends a written indication of interest and at least two wholesalers agree to pilot.
