# Pull-Through Platform: AI-enabled fire and life-safety ITM contractors

**One-liner:** buy owner-run fire inspection, testing and maintenance (ITM) contractors and run an AI "deficiency desk" that turns inspection findings into billed repair work. A cross-branch pull-through overlay for PE roll-ups is the software variant.

**Verdict:** this is an AI-enabled services roll-up, not a software company. It is the best fit for an acquirer and a weak fit for the default technical outsider. It sits below the 70 bar under every profile, because nobody has yet shown how large the AI uplift is, and because entry multiples and repair labor are already tight.

## Thesis
In fire ITM, the inspection is the loss leader and the repair work it uncovers ("pull-through") is the profit. ServiceTrade, a vendor, says contractors in the top half on pull-through earn 99% more per customer. The obvious software product, "AI turns the inspection report into a priced quote," is already taken: ServiceTrade Inspections (June 2025), Deelo, IRONBACK (45 minutes down to 3 per quote), ZenFire and BuildOps all ship it. What is still open is **owning the pull-through P&L**: buy contractors with large inspection bases and weak conversion, then use AI to draft proposals, bundle quotes, time follow-ups to AHJ (the local fire authority) deadlines and pre-quote NFPA time-based items. The owner keeps the whole repair margin and then sells into a very active buyer market. Pye-Barker alone did 57 acquisitions in 2025.

## Workflow today
1. **Inspect.** A NICET or state-licensed tech inspects under NFPA 25, 72, 10, 96 and 2001, using a tablet app or paper. Note quality varies widely ("heads obstructed, 3rd fl").
2. **Report.** The report goes to the owner or property manager, and more and more often to the AHJ portal (The Compliance Engine). Contractors pay $10-37 per report, and the portal sends automated outreach to owners.
3. **Quote.** This is the bottleneck. A coordinator decodes the note, calls the tech, prices parts and labor, and types the quote into the FSM, QuickBooks or Excel.
4. **Approve.** Quotes above the property manager's signing limit trigger multiple bids or capital approval. Follow-up is sporadic, and non-critical items die here.
5. **Repair.** Parts are ordered and a scarce repair tech is scheduled. The repair is filed with the AHJ and the work is invoiced.
6. **Forecastable work.** NFPA time-based replacements are often found ad hoc instead of being pre-quoted from the asset register.

## TAM (assumptions labelled)
- **End market:** about 5.9M US commercial buildings (CBECS 2018). Assume 3-4M carry contractor-serviced fire systems, at $2-3k a year of inspection spend each. That gives roughly **$7-12B in inspection** and **$8-15B in repair** (the repair ratio is folklore). Vendor estimates put fire and life-safety services at $22-32B.
- **Leakage:** assume 20-40% of found deficiencies never get billed, which is **$2-5B a year**. This number is unverified.
- **Software pool:** fire FSM software is about $0.3-0.6B. A deficiency-to-quote module alone is $50-150M, too small to stand alone. An outcome-fee pool would be $35-170M before attribution disputes.
- **Ownership pool (C):** the full repair gross margin. A $10M contractor adding $1-2M of repair revenue at 40-50% gross margin is the unit case. The services TAM runs to tens of billions.

## Wedge and path to scale
- **C (recommended):** buy one inspection-heavy contractor with $3-10M revenue, a retiring owner, paper or a legacy FSM, in a market where TCE filing is required.
  - Measure 24 months of deficiencies found, quoted and sold.
  - Install the AI desk: drafted proposals with citations a human reviews, bundling under signing limits, follow-up timed to AHJ deadlines, pre-quoted NFPA calendar items, and repairs batched onto inspection routes.
  - **Success test:** +15 points of pull-through within 12 months.
  - Then tuck in 10-15 firms toward $100M revenue (about $15-20M EBITDA, worth $150-240M at 10-12x). Expand into backflow, kitchen hood and generator inspection. Software built in-house can be licensed out later.
- **A:** sell the overlay top-down to 1-3 PE platforms: a league table of branch pull-through plus a proposal agent. Realistic outcome is $10-40M ARR and a sale to an FSM or a platform.
- **B:** an outsourced regional deficiency desk for 10-30 independents, priced per proposal or at 3-5% of booked work.
- **0:** a free leakage audit on FSM exports only, which is not defensible.

## Steelman summary
- Owning the contractor turns a crowded SaaS feature into an internal tool that is cheap to buy or build. The fact that incumbents have to sell "deficiency efficiency" shows contractors are not capturing the value on their own.
- Multiple arbitrage exists before any AI effect. Lower-middle-market firms sell at about 4-7x, inspection-heavy platforms at 8-12x+, and the largest deals at 17-20x.
- The thesis can be underwritten deal by deal, because the target's own found, quoted and sold data proves or disproves leakage before close.
- More than $3B of "AI-enabled roll-up" capital (General Catalyst, Thrive) has no fire deal yet, which leaves room.
- NFPA schedules and AHJ enforcement keep demand coming.
- Steelman score for C: 68.

## Skeptic summary
- **Competition and go-to-market:** every software objection disappears under C, but deal competition is serious. About 125 fire and life-safety deals closed in 2025, up 67%, and 46% were PE add-ons. Small deals cleared about 5.5-6.7x EBITDA in H1 2025. The return ends up rewarding ordinary roll-up execution more than the AI lever.
- **Feasibility, regulation and capital:**
  - Inspection-heavy firms, which are exactly the target, trade at **8-12x**, not 4-7x.
  - Repair labor is the binding constraint: a 14-20% skilled shortfall, and 26% of techs near retirement.
  - Vague tech notes and local NFPA amendments keep a human estimator in the loop.
  - An AI that regrades or splits deficiencies creates discoverable liability records.
  - State licenses often sit with a qualifying individual, frequently the retiring owner.
  - Reaching $100M means $80-150M of purchase price.
- Skeptic scores for C: 56-62.

**Weighing:** the skeptics are right that the 4-7x assumption is too rosy for inspection-heavy targets, and that labor is a real constraint. The steelman is right that small, owner-run firms still trade near 5.5x, and that the thesis can be checked target by target. Final C score: **60**, between the bull and bear cases.

## What's good
- The value pool is large and verifiable from target data before any capital is committed.
- The acquirer keeps 100% of incremental margin. There are no attribution, integration or feature-risk problems.
- The exit market is proven and deep, with a sector-average multiple of about 11.8x.
- Regulation drives the demand: NFPA cadence plus AHJ portal enforcement.
- Incumbent tooling makes the AI desk cheap to assemble.

## What's bad
- The entry price for the best targets (inspection-heavy) is already 8-12x, and 125 deals a year means crowded sourcing.
- Repair-tech scarcity can turn converted quotes into backlog rather than EBITDA.
- The size of the AI uplift has not been proven anywhere, and the 20-40% leakage rate is folklore.
- Liability and licensing burden: discoverable AI quote records, and licenses tied to a qualifying individual.
- The software variants (0 and A) face shipped incumbents and a shrinking pool of overlay buyers.

## Best founder profile and why
**C, or a search fund partnered with a fire operator.** Owning the contractor eliminates every software objection: feature risk, FSM dependence, attribution and SMB sales. The remaining risks (multiple, labor, licensing) are standard acquisition-diligence items and can be priced per deal. To clear 70, C needs evidence from 2-3 real targets showing low baseline pull-through *and* spare repair capacity, ideally from owner-run, install-heavy shops bought at 4-5x and converted toward ITM.

## First 30 days (profile C)
1. **Week 1:** build a target list of 40-60 owner-run fire contractors ($3-10M revenue) in TCE-mandated metros. Screen for owner age, paper or legacy FSM, and inspection share.
2. **Week 1-2:** interview 10 service coordinators and 5 operations leaders, including ex-Pye-Barker staff. Validate leakage by category (critical vs. non-critical vs. time-based), the property-manager signing-limit effect, and the repair-capacity gap.
3. **Week 2-3:** under NDA, get FSM exports from 2-3 sellers. Compute found, quoted and sold rates and repair-tech utilization. Prototype the proposal agent on real notes and measure what share needs a callback to the tech.
4. **Week 3-4:** get broker reads on actual multiples for inspection-heavy versus install-heavy shops. Map state licensing and qualifier transfer. Check E&O insurance for AI-assisted quoting.
5. **Go/no-go:** baseline pull-through below 60%, repair utilization below 80%, and an entry price of 6x or less. If any one fails, pivot to the B desk or stop.
